## C. Development and Phase History

### Verified history

- **College Hills plat (1914):**  
  - A plat called **College Hills** was created around **1914**, showing lot numbers, dimensions, and early sales annotated.[3]  
  - It covered what is now part of Shorewood Hills; streets were named after American/English colleges to appeal to university‑affiliated buyers.[5]  
- **Developer and vision:**  
  - In 1913, **John C. McKenna** formed the **University Bay Land Co.**, purchased farmland with views of Lake Mendota, Picnic Point, and the UW campus, and platted **College Hills**.[5]  
  - Advertising emphasized “high class homes” and elite, educated residents, enforced by deed restrictions.[5]  
- **Expansion to “Shorewood”:**  
  - As demand grew after World War I, McKenna bought an additional strip of land and platted **Shorewood** as a companion subdivision.[5]  
  - The **Village of Shorewood Hills** was then incorporated, encompassing both College Hills and Shorewood as a self‑governing suburb.[5][13]  
- **Major build‑out periods:**  
  - College Hills Historic District contributing buildings date **1914–1959**, indicating that much of the village’s housing stock was built across that period.[5]  
  - On the west side, the **Shorewood Historic District** was platted 1922–1926 on Mendota Heights hill, with homes constructed between **1924–1963**, in various architectural styles.[12]  
  - “Although a number of homes were built between the wars, it was not until after the Second World War that the majority of houses were built.”[5]  

### Phases and later additions

- The village’s historic documentation frames development in terms of **historic districts** (College Hills, Shorewood Historic District) rather than numbered phases. Plats from **1914 onward** show the incremental expansion of streets and lots.[3][5][12]

### Original land use

- The area was originally **farmland** overlooking Lake Mendota; McKenna’s University Bay Land Co. purchased this farmland and platted residential lots.[5]  
- The plat was marketed specifically to the growing **university‑related population**, using the term “University Bay” and college street names.[5]
